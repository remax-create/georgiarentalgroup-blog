Georgia Tenants Are Staying Longer: Why Renewals Pay

Sandra Watkins, Broker/Owner November 24, 2026
Share Facebook LinkedIn X Email

Here's a question I get from owners every renewal season: "The rent down the street is higher. Should I bump my tenant up to match?" Sometimes the answer is yes. A lot of the time, the smarter money move is a fair increase and a signed renewal. Let me show you why.

The short version: Renters are staying put longer, more rentals are sitting empty, and one turnover can cost you more than a year of rent increases brings in. Price renewals on what like-kind homes actually leased for, keep the increase fair, and keep the good tenant.

Renters are staying longer, and that's good news for owners

Georgia renters aren't moving like they used to. Based on U.S. Census data, the share of Georgia renters who moved in a year dropped from 28.6% in 2014 to 24.3% in 2024. People are settling in.

That's an opportunity. A tenant who pays on time, takes care of the home and wants to stay is the most valuable thing your rental has. Every year they renew is a year you skip vacancy, make-ready and leasing costs.

Empty rentals are sitting longer right now

At the same time, there's more competition for renters. The U.S. Census Bureau reported the national rental vacancy rate rose to 7.6% in the third quarter of 2026, up from 7.1% a year earlier. Rents have cooled too: Zumper's November report had Atlanta's one-bedroom median at $1,600, down more than 6% from last year, and November kicks off the slow months for renting.

Translation: if your tenant moves out this winter, your home may sit longer than you expect, and you may not get the higher rent you were counting on.

For perspective, as of this week 96% of the homes we manage are leased. That's a vacancy rate under 4%, about half the national rate.

The real math: one turnover vs. a fair renewal

Take a $2,000 a month rental. If the tenant leaves, a typical turnover looks something like this:

  • One month vacant: $2,000 in lost rent, and longer in the slow season.
  • Leasing fee: with us, that's the first month's rent, another $2,000.
  • Make-ready: cleaning, paint touch-ups and small repairs, easily $750 or more.

That's about $4,750 before the new tenant even moves in. Now compare a fair $100 a month renewal increase: $1,200 more over the year, with no vacancy, no leasing fee and a tenant whose payment history you already know. Pushing a good tenant out to chase an extra $100 can take years to break even. Your numbers will be different, but the pattern almost always holds.

How we price a renewal

Here's how I do it for every owner we manage for:

  • About 90 days out, I send you the lease end date, the current rent and a rent analysis with my suggestion.
  • The analysis is based on like-kind homes that actually leased recently. Not one shiny listing that's still sitting on Zillow, and never a single property. Active listings show what someone is hoping to get, not what the market paid.
  • We look at your tenant, too: on-time payments, how they treat the home, and what a turnover would really cost you.
  • Then the renewal offer goes to the tenant with plenty of notice, so everyone has time to plan.

Sometimes the right call is a solid increase. Sometimes it's a small one. And once in a while it's no increase at all, because the numbers say keeping that tenant is worth more. You'll always see the comps and the reasoning.

Small things that keep good tenants renewing

  • Fix things fast. Nothing sends a tenant shopping like a repair request that sits for two weeks.
  • Communicate clearly. No surprises on rent, fees or inspections.
  • Give notice early. A renewal offer that shows up months ahead feels respectful. One that shows up two weeks before move-out feels like a squeeze.
  • Keep increases fair. A reasonable bump with a clear reason gets signed. A big jump with no explanation gets a 30-day notice.

Quick answers

Should I raise rent on a good tenant?
Often yes, modestly. Base it on what like-kind homes actually leased for recently, and weigh it against what a turnover would cost you.

How much does a turnover really cost?
On a $2,000 a month rental, one vacant month, a leasing fee and a basic make-ready can add up to roughly $4,750. Slow-season vacancies can run longer.

When should renewal talks start?
We start with owners about 90 days before the lease ends, so the tenant gets the offer well ahead of time.
